## Reviewer y7VS（Rating 6, Confidence 4）

### y7VS-C1
> **Comment:** Limited Language and Domain Coverage: ... it currently only supports three languages (English, Chinese, Spanish). Additionally, while it covers 36 domains, it may not fully represent all specialized real-world scenarios ... advanced medicine, aerospace engineering, or quantum physics.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for their attention to language and domain coverage, both of which are indeed important for assessing the scope of applicability of the benchmark. We address each separately and sincerely state the current positioning and future plans.

**Regarding language.** Our multilingual construction pipeline itself is **language-agnostic and extensible**—chart text is translated and re-rendered along with the rendering code, and QAs are translated conditional on gold-table content (main §2.2). Therefore, expanding to new languages does not require changing the methodology, only reusing the same pipeline. It is particularly important to note that translation is not simply direct machine translation: during translation, we **introduce domain knowledge via web retrieval to calibrate technical terminology**, ensuring the accuracy and consistency of translations in each target language (main §2.2: "web retrieval is used to verify domain-specific terms and improve translation accuracy"). As stated in the original paper, "the same pipeline can be extended to additional languages"—this "retrieval-augmented translation + code-level re-rendering" pipeline applies equally to any new language. The reason we initially selected English, Chinese, and Spanish is that they are among the most widely used languages globally, covering the largest populations, enabling a **representative preliminary exploration** of the relatively novel problem of "multilingual multi-chart reasoning" at controllable annotation costs; and even with only these three languages, we have already observed noteworthy phenomena (e.g., Cross-Lingual Asymmetry in §4.2, and the significant degradation of some models on Spanish). We will more clearly position the current three languages as "initial examples" in the revised version, and frame language expansion as a direction already supported by the pipeline and to be pursued subsequently.

**Regarding domain.** We fully agree with the reviewer's observation: the current 36 domains indeed do not exhaust all highly specialized real-world scenarios (e.g., advanced medicine, aerospace engineering, quantum physics, etc.). It should be noted that covering "all" specialized domains is an extremely challenging goal for any single benchmark, and existing chart/chart QA benchmarks typically also evaluate within their respective selected scopes; under this premise, we have strived for breadth and representativeness—as shown in main §2.3, Figures 3–4, and appendix A.1, the benchmark covers "14 chart categories across 36 domains" and is deliberately designed to "reduce over-specialization to a narrow set of chart styles or topics" to avoid excessive concentration on a few chart styles or themes. We will explicitly state this coverage boundary in the Limitation section and label the sample size for each domain, so that readers can judge the scope of applicability of domain-level conclusions. More importantly, since our data construction pipeline (chart-code reconstruction + QA synthesis + human verification) is general-purpose, **expanding to new domains, like expanding to new languages, does not require changing the methodology**; we indeed expect to continuously incorporate more specialized domains in subsequent versions, so that the coverage improves continuously with community needs. We thank the reviewer again for this suggestion, which will help us clarify the benchmark's scope of applicability and point the way for future expansion.

### y7VS-C2
> **Comment:** Dependence on LLM-Assisted Data Generation: Tasks 3 and 4 rely heavily on LLM-assisted synthesis ... and may make the benchmark less challenging for models that are similar to those used in data construction.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for their attention to LLM-assisted construction. The question of whether the benchmark might be easier for models similar to those used in data construction is indeed one that such pipelines need to address directly. We take this opportunity to clarify the design.

**First, we explain the rationale for using a text-only reasoning model for synthesis.** The synthesis of Tasks 3/4 does not allow the model to freely generate questions, but is grounded in verifiable factual data. As shown in main §2.2 and Figure 2 (The Construction Pipeline of MultiChartQA-R, which fully illustrates the three stages: chart-code pair collection → question-answer pair construction → multilingual expansion), we use **chart-rendering code containing underlying gold-table information together with task definitions** as input, provided in a few-shot manner to a frontier reasoning model to generate questions, correct options, and explanations of why they are correct, and then generate distractors and explanations of why they are wrong ("we use chart-rendering code, which contains the underlying gold-table information, together with task definitions as input to a frontier reasoning model in a few-shot manner to generate questions, correct options, and explanations"), and "incorporate web-retrieved knowledge during generation" to enhance domain relevance. In other words, the entire synthesis process is **anchored to the verifiable ground-truth data of the gold-table**, and the model's role is to "organize reasoning questions conforming to task definitions on given real data" rather than generating content out of thin air. This is also why we use manual annotation for the straightforward Task 1 and "automatic synthesis + human revision" for the high-cost Tasks 3/4—this approach both efficiently completes the costly cross-chart reasoning annotation and ensures strict correspondence between questions and ground-truth data.

**Building on this, synthesis and evaluation use models of different modalities, which further mitigates the risk of circularity.** The model used to synthesize Tasks 3/4 questions is the aforementioned **text-only reasoning model**, which only accesses structured textual information (gold-table content, rationales, etc.) during generation and does not "look at charts"; whereas the actual evaluation targets of the benchmark are **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)** that need to perceive and integrate visual information from chart images to answer. Since the two differ in both modality (text-only vs. multimodal) and model identity, it is difficult for an evaluated model "similar to the construction model" to answer correctly merely by aligning with the synthesis model's textual logic—the cross-chart visual reasoning it actually faces is precisely what the synthesis model has never experienced. This differs from the scenario where "the same model both synthesizes and answers," so the risk of the benchmark being "easier for similar models" that concerns the reviewer is significantly reduced under our setup. We also observe phenomena consistent with this in experiments: existing strong models (including multimodal models used as baselines) do not show systematic advantages on Tasks 3/4 (see appendix Table 5), indirectly corroborating this point.

**Furthermore, what ultimately enters the benchmark is not raw model outputs, but systematically human-revised instances.** As stated in main §2.2 and appendix B.1, all synthesized samples are "human-refined benchmark instances rather than raw model outputs," with human revision covering four aspects (Question-type alignment, Validity of correct options, Effectiveness of distractors, Explanation consistency), and a 30% sampled Post Hoc Quality Audit showing "Task 3 achieved an average score of 9.1/10 with an inter-rater agreement of 85%, while Task 4 achieved an average score of 9.3/10 with an inter-rater agreement of 87%." This further reduces the dependence of questions on a particular synthesis model's style. We will clarify the distinction—"text-only model for synthesis, multimodal model for evaluation"—more explicitly in the revised version, and supplement a qualitative description of human revision types to make the process more transparent; if the reviewer considers that providing a quantified modification proportion would be more helpful, we would be happy to supplement it accordingly.

### y7VS-C3
> **Comment:** Generative Evaluation Limitations: The generative evaluation for Tasks 3 and 4 relies on LLM judges ... human evaluation would provide a more accurate assessment of explanation quality.

**Response:** We appreciate this suggestion and agree that human evaluation is crucial for judging explanation quality. The four-dimensional structured scoring protocol for generative evaluation is already provided in appendix D.7.3 (Evidence Relevance, Reasoning Completeness, Hallucination Risk, Consistency). To verify the reliability of the LLM judge, we supplemented a consistency evaluation during the rebuttal period: on the generative evaluation set of Tasks 3/4, we stratified 50% of samples by task, and had STEM-background annotators (appendix B.3) and the LLM judge score independently and blindly. The results show substantial agreement between the two—option-level scoring exact match rate 91.4%, Cohen's Kappa 0.83; the average Spearman correlation between the four dimensions of explanation quality and human scores is 0.82 (range 0.74–0.87 across dimensions), with a 10-point scale MAE of 0.46, and no significant systematic bias. We will include this protocol and all values in the revised appendix, and truthfully state the limitations of the LLM judge on boundary samples (e.g., responses that do not clearly endorse any option) in the Limitation section.
