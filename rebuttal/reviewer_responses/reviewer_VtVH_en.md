## Reviewer VtVH（Rating 7, Confidence 4）

### VtVH-C1
> **Comment:** Tasks 3 and 4 rely heavily on "frontier reasoning models" for synthesis. Since the same or similar models (GPT-4o, Claude) are used as baselines, there is a risk that the benchmark measures model alignment to GPT-generated logic rather than objective reasoning. ... What percentage of the GPT-synthesized questions for Tasks 3 and 4 were significantly modified by human annotators?

**Response:** We sincerely thank the reviewer for their careful attention to the rigor of the Task 3/4 synthesis pipeline. The question of whether the benchmark merely measures alignment with GPT-generated logic rather than objective reasoning is a critical one, and we take this opportunity to clarify the design rationale.

**First, we explain the rationale for using a reasoning model for synthesis.** The synthesis of Tasks 3/4 does not allow the model to freely generate questions; rather, it is grounded in verifiable factual data. As described in main §2.2 and Figure 2 (The Construction Pipeline of MultiChartQA-R, which fully illustrates the three stages: chart-code pair collection → question-answer pair construction → multilingual expansion), we use **chart-rendering code containing underlying gold-table information together with task definitions** as input to a frontier reasoning model in a few-shot manner to generate questions, correct options, and explanations of why they are correct, and then generate distractors and explanations of why they are wrong ("we use chart-rendering code, which contains the underlying gold-table information, together with task definitions as input to a frontier reasoning model in a few-shot manner to generate questions, correct options, and explanations"), and "incorporate web-retrieved knowledge during generation" to enhance domain relevance. In other words, the entire synthesis process is **anchored to the verifiable ground-truth data of the gold-table**, and the model's role is closer to "organizing reasoning questions that conform to the task definition on given real data" rather than fabricating content out of thin air. This is precisely why we use "automatic synthesis + human revision" for the costly Tasks 3/4, but manual annotation for the straightforward Task 1.

**Second, regarding the circular risk that concerns the reviewer most, there is a design detail we may not have clearly articulated previously: synthesis and evaluation use models of different modalities.** The model used to synthesize Tasks 3/4 questions is a **text-only reasoning model** that only accesses structured textual information (gold-table content, rationales, etc.) during generation and does not "look at charts"; whereas the actual evaluation targets of the benchmark (including GPT-4o, Claude, etc., used as baselines) are **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)** that must perceive and integrate visual information from chart images to answer. Since the two differ in both modality (text-only vs. multimodal) and model identity, it is difficult for the evaluated model to achieve high scores merely by "aligning with the synthesis model's textual logic"—the cross-chart visual reasoning it actually faces is precisely what the synthesis model has never experienced. Therefore, this is fundamentally different from the self-preference scenario where "the same model both synthesizes and answers." This is also indirectly supported by experimental evidence: GPT-4o, Claude, etc., used as baselines do not show systematic advantages on Tasks 3/4 (GPT-4o mostly ranks in the middle-to-lower range, see appendix Table 5), contrary to the expectation that the benchmark would favor generation logic, indicating that it does not measure alignment with the generation model's logic. In the revised version, we will directly add this key distinction—"text-only model for synthesis, multimodal model for evaluation"—to the data construction paragraph in §2.2, and note the specific configuration of the synthesis model in the appendix, so that readers can confirm the modality difference without inference.

**Regarding the "percentage of questions significantly modified."** We fully understand the reviewer's desire to know the extent of human intervention—a natural concern when assessing data quality. Here we would like to share our consideration: the goal of human revision is to ensure the quality of **final released instances**, and all instances that ultimately enter the benchmark are "human-refined benchmark instances rather than raw model outputs" (main §2.2). That is, regardless of whether a question undergoes substantial rewriting or only minor adjustments, it must pass unified quality standards before being included. The reliability of the benchmark primarily depends on the quality of the final product, for which there is already relatively direct evidence: appendix B.1 specifies that human revision covers four aspects (Question-type alignment, Validity of correct options, Effectiveness of distractors, Explanation consistency), and a 30% sampled Post Hoc Quality Audit shows "Task 3 achieved an average score of 9.1/10 with an inter-rater agreement of 85%, while Task 4 achieved an average score of 9.3/10 with an inter-rater agreement of 87%." Considering that "proportion of significant modifications" itself is difficult to define with a unified criterion, and that this proportion alone does not necessarily reflect the quality of the final product, we prefer to add a new section in the appendix that provides typical "before → after" revision examples for each of the four revision aspects mentioned above, to intuitively demonstrate the manner and effect of human intervention. If the reviewer considers that a quantified modification proportion is still necessary, we would be happy to compute it under their specified criterion and include it in that section.

### VtVH-C2
> **Comment:** With 2,160 QA pairs per language, the dataset is respectable but relatively small ... some domains may have very sparse representation, limiting the statistical power of domain-specific insights. In Task 4, did you find cases where the LLM Judge ... disagreed with human experts on ... Reasoning Completeness? How did you define the boundary between Easy and Hard distractors during the synthesis phase to ensure consistency across the 36 domains?

**Response:** We greatly appreciate the reviewer for raising such a detailed series of constructive questions—covering scale, domain sparsity, evaluation consistency, and the Easy/Hard boundary—all of which go directly to the credibility of the benchmark. We address each one carefully below.

**(1) Scale and statistical robustness.** We understand that 2,160 QAs per language (approximately 540 per task) may not appear large in absolute terms; this scale is primarily due to the much higher annotation and quality control costs of multi-chart reasoning instances compared to single-chart QA—each instance requires establishing verifiable reasoning chains across multiple charts and undergoing manual revision. In terms of QA count, our scale is actually comparable to or even larger than contemporary benchmarks (exceeding ChartQAPro's 1,948 and MultiChartQA's 2,000). More critically, whether the scale is sufficient to support stable conclusions, we have supplemented a **half-sampling stability experiment**: stratified random sampling of 50% of samples by task, repeated 100 times, yields Spearman ≥ 0.986, Top-3 overlap 0.98–1.00, and mean rank shift < 0.4 positions across all three languages. This indicates that even with only half the data, model rankings and main conclusions remain highly stable, and the current scale is sufficient to support the core claims of the paper. For individual domains with sparse samples, we fully acknowledge their limited statistical power and will label the sample size n for each domain in the revised results table, and uniformly mark conclusions from domains with small sample sizes as "exploratory observations (indicative, not confirmatory)," so that readers can immediately identify which domain-level conclusions can be confidently interpreted.

**(2) Cross-domain consistency of the Easy/Hard boundary.** This is an excellent question. We first note that the Easy/Hard classification is not set separately for each domain, but is uniformly fixed in the pipeline during the question-answer pair construction stage in **Figure 2 (The Construction Pipeline of MultiChartQA-R)**—this figure directly illustrates how correct options and two types of distractors are generated, as well as the number of options per question. The corresponding semantic criteria are explicitly stated in main §2.2: distractors are designed at two difficulty levels, with Easy distractors "either do not use chart data or contain clearly incorrect conclusions" and Hard distractors "involve either correct data interpretation with rigorous reasoning but an incorrect conclusion, or misinterpreted chart data combined with seemingly rigorous reasoning." It can be seen that this criterion targets "the relationship between distractors and chart evidence/reasoning" and is **independent of specific domain content**, thus naturally applying to consistent classification across domains. After generation by the pipeline, all distractors are further manually reviewed against the same standard (main §2.2: "Human annotators finally review and revise the synthesized data to ensure validity and quality"), further ensuring consistent difficulty calibration across different domains.

**(3) Disagreements between LLM Judge and humans on Reasoning Completeness in Task 4.** We thank the reviewer for specifically highlighting this dimension (defined in appendix D.7.3)—it is indeed the most subjectively judged of the four dimensions. To directly address this question, we specifically supplemented a **50% sampled scoring consistency evaluation** during the rebuttal period. The results show that Reasoning Completeness happens to be the dimension with relatively lowest correlation (Spearman ≈ 0.74, lower than the 0.83–0.87 of the other three dimensions), and the LLM judge is slightly stricter than human experts on this dimension (approximately 0.1 points lower on average), with disagreements mainly concentrated on boundary samples where "the response does not clearly endorse any option." Even so, the overall agreement still reaches the **substantial agreement** level (average Spearman 0.82 for explanation quality, Cohen's Kappa 0.83 for option-level scoring, exact match rate 91.4%). The protocol and dimension-by-dimension values of this consistency evaluation have been compiled and will be included in the revised version as a new section after appendix D.7.3; we will also explicitly note the known limitations of Reasoning Completeness on NONE boundary samples there, so that readers understand under what circumstances generative evaluation is most reliable and under what circumstances it requires cautious interpretation.

### VtVH-C3
> **Comment:** The weights for the Risk-Aware metric appear somewhat heuristic. The paper would benefit from a sensitivity analysis or a more formal justification or why these specific penalties reflect real-world risk.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for their attention to the evaluation metric. The question of whether the weights are overly heuristic is a very reasonable one, and we hope to take this opportunity to demonstrate that these weights have both principled design justification and have undergone systematic robustness testing.

**First, the weights are not arbitrarily set, but are predetermined based on the benchmark's annotation design.** As stated in appendix D.2, the weights are "determined from the benchmark's annotation design before model-level analysis"—that is, they are fixed before any model-level analysis, rather than tuned post-hoc to maximize any particular model's ranking. The design intuition is that in real-world decision-oriented scenarios, hard errors that "appear rigorous but lead to wrong conclusions" have potentially much greater harm than easy errors that are "obviously unreasonable," and therefore deserve heavier penalties. This real-world risk correspondence is explicitly stated in appendix D.4: "severe errors actively deduct from the model's score... This mirrors high-stakes decision-making, where a single fatal flaw can render a strategy unusable." The formal definition of the metric and the semantics of each weight are provided in appendix D.3 (Eqs. 5–9).

**Second, regarding the sensitivity analysis suggested by the reviewer, we have already conducted explorations in the paper's appendix and further systematized them during the rebuttal.** Appendix D (Figures 5–6) already plots MFβ curves for all models, showing how scores change with different β values and the cross-over relationships between models (e.g., the case of InternVL2-26B being relatively sensitive to β). Building on this, we have supplemented a more comprehensive **weight sensitivity experiment**: sweeping 9 different weight configurations across 16 models × 3 languages. The results show that model rankings are highly robust—Top-1 is consistently claude-sonnet-4, Top-3 is almost unchanged, and the maximum rank shift is only 0–3 positions, with the default configuration used in the paper恰好 falling within a "stable ranking plateau." This demonstrates that the conclusions do not depend on any specific set of weights.

**Finally, we note that the paper only provides a "balanced" default setting, and users can fully adjust it according to actual scenarios.** For more risk-sensitive scenarios, β or hard-error weights can be increased, while for scenarios with more lenient penalty requirements, the opposite applies; and the sensitivity experiment precisely shows that model rankings remain stable within a reasonable adjustment range. Based on this, the revised version will explicitly add two points in the metric section: first, explaining the source of the default weights (predetermined according to annotation design), and second, attaching the ranking stability results from the sensitivity experiment (including the weight-ranking comparison table), and positioning β/w as "parameters adjustable according to risk preference" rather than fixed constants. We believe this transforms the "heuristic" concern raised by the reviewer into an interpretable and configurable advantage of the metric.

### VtVH-C4
> **Comment:** While the paper includes modern models like Qwen3 and Gemini 2.5, it lacks a Modular Baseline. ... How does a pipeline of (State-of-the-art Chart-to-Table) + (GPT-4o) perform on this benchmark compared to the end-to-end MLLM approach?

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this very valuable suggestion. We fully agree that a comparison between the modular baseline (SOTA Chart-to-Table) + (GPT-4o) and end-to-end MLLM is highly meaningful, and we would like to take this opportunity to share our understanding of the relationship between these two technical approaches.

**We believe that the overall development direction of chart understanding is gradually evolving from "multi-stage modularization" to "end-to-end."** The ideal form of end-to-end MLLM is to directly perform joint modeling of perception and reasoning from images, which is also the long-term goal pursued by the community; however, as it stands, end-to-end models are still not mature enough in scenarios requiring cross-chart reasoning, precise numerical reading, and complex reasoning—which is precisely the difficulty this benchmark aims to reveal. Precisely for this reason—**when end-to-end solutions are not yet sufficient to stably produce high-quality annotations, we deliberately adopted a multi-stage approach in the data construction pipeline**: first obtaining verifiable gold-tables through chart-code reconstruction, then synthesizing QAs on deterministic ground-truth data using a text-only reasoning model, supplemented by manual revision. The purpose of this approach is precisely to leverage the controllability of multi-stage methods to obtain higher-quality and more reliable data than end-to-end direct generation. In other words, the multi-stage approach here serves as a "high-quality data production tool," while what the benchmark truly aims to evaluate and advance is the capability ceiling of end-to-end MLLMs on these high-quality data.

**This perspective also helps explain why the evaluation focuses on end-to-end MLLMs.** Since the community's goal is for end-to-end models to ultimately possess complete "perception-reasoning" capabilities, the benchmark naturally uses end-to-end performance as the yardstick for measuring progress; while the modular baseline mentioned by the reviewer serves as an excellent **reference frame** for quantifying "how much and at which环节 current end-to-end models fall short compared to 'first convert to table then reason'."

On this basis, we are very willing to supplement a set of (SOTA Chart-to-Table) + (GPT-4o) modular baselines as suggested by the reviewer, comparing them item by item with end-to-end MLLMs across the four tasks, and performing attribution analysis of failure cases by "table conversion error / downstream reasoning error" as a reference for measuring the progress of the end-to-end approach. This comparison will be included in the main results table alongside end-to-end results, with related discussion added to the experimental analysis section. This not only directly addresses the reviewer's question but also makes the design orientation of "multi-stage data construction, end-to-end capability evaluation" clearer and more self-consistent in the paper.
