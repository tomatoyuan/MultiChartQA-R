## Reviewer 9osS（Rating 5, Confidence 4）

### 9osS-C1
> **Comment:** Although the Strict Risk-Aware metric is motivated and easy to understand, it is still relatively simple. Could the authors provide a sensitivity analysis showing how rankings change under different weight configurations?

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. Sensitivity analysis can indeed more forcefully demonstrate the robustness of the metric, and we have accordingly supplemented relevant experiments, which we explain below together with existing explorations already in the paper's appendix.

**(1) New weight sensitivity experiment (Experiment 1).** Using the paper's configuration `e1_h0.5` (`w_e=1.0`, `w_h=0.5`, `β=1`) as the baseline, we swept **9 weight configurations** across all **16 models** and **3 languages** (varying along two axes: easy/hard severity weights and β), recomputing MFβ scores and rankings for all models for each configuration, and comparing Top-1, Top-3 membership, and maximum absolute rank shift against the baseline. The results show highly robust rankings: **Top-1 is consistently claude-sonnet-4 across all 9 configurations and 3 languages, without exception**; **the Top-3 set is almost entirely unchanged** (only under the most extreme configuration do Qwen3-VL-32B and gemini-2.5-pro swap 3rd place); **the maximum absolute rank shift is only 0–1 under most configurations**, and even the most extreme `e0.5_h0.5` reaches only 3 in en, max 1 in cn, and max 2 in es. This demonstrates that the baseline configuration was not chosen because it is "special," but because it falls within a **stable ranking plateau**.

**(2) The influence of β has already been systematically explored in the original paper's appendix.** This part further reinforces the reviewer's concerns (see appendix.pdf, Appendix D *Metric Exploration*, pp. 11–13): D.3 (Eqs. 5–9) provides the complete definition of MFβ with fixed severity weights; D.4 provides the semantics of β (`β=1` balances precision and recall equally, `β>1` places greater emphasis on avoiding incorrect selections, `β<1` places greater emphasis on covering correct options); D.5 "Analysis of MFβ Curves" plots MFβ curves for all models across different β values in **Figure 5, Figure 6**, and analyzes the trade-off between "recalling correct items vs. avoiding incorrect items" for different models and its guiding significance for scenario-based model selection through curve **intersection points** (using InternVL2-26B as an example to illustrate that some models are highly sensitive to β).

**(3) The default configuration is a "balanced" setting that can be adjusted according to scenarios.** We emphasize that `e1_h0.5, β=1` is only a default setting that balances intuitive reasonableness and ranking stability, not the only correct configuration: for high-risk/high-compliance scenarios (e.g., medical, financial), β can be increased to more heavily penalize incorrect selections and favor precision; for exploratory scenarios, β can be decreased to prioritize recall; `w_e` and `w_h` can also be adjusted according to business judgments on the harm of the two error types. The sensitivity experiment further demonstrates that model relative rankings remain robust within the above adjustable range. Complete configurations, data, and charts can be found in [`supplementary_experiments.md`](./supplementary_experiments.md); we will formally include this sensitivity analysis table in the revised appendix, immediately following the existing MFβ curve analysis.

### 9osS-C2
> **Comment:** The dataset is small: 2,160 QA pairs per language, 180 multi-chart sets, 695 charts. Can you explain what led to this size? Does it affect the reliability of your findings? Bootstrap confidence intervals would help.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for their attention to data scale and its reliability. We address these two points separately below.

**(1) Scale composition and its causes.** As stated in main Table 1 and appendix A.1, each language version contains "180 multi-chart sets, 695 chart-code pairs, and 2,160 QA pairs, with 540 instances for each of the four tasks," and "On average, each set contains 3.9 charts, reflecting the realistic need to aggregate evidence across multiple visualizations." The scale is primarily determined by the higher annotation and quality control costs of multi-chart reasoning instances—unlike single-chart QA, each instance requires establishing verifiable reasoning chains across multiple charts and undergoing complete manual revision and quality inspection (appendix B.1). It should be noted that, in terms of QA count for horizontal comparison, our 2,160 is not smaller than contemporary work, exceeding ChartQAPro (1,948) and MultiChartQA (2,000).

**(2) Whether scale affects conclusion reliability—half-sampling stability experiment (Experiment 2).** We fully agree that the key is not absolute quantity, but whether the scale is sufficient to support stable conclusions. To this end, we have supplemented a stability experiment: **stratified random sampling of 50% of samples by task**, **repeated 100 times**, to examine the fluctuation of model rankings relative to full-sample results. The results show highly stable rankings across all three languages—**Spearman correlation ≥ 0.986**, **Top-3 overlap rate 0.98–1.00**, **mean rank shift < 0.4 positions**, with an average score deviation of only approximately 0.7–1.1 points (on a 100-point scale), far smaller than the true score gaps between models. This indicates that even with half the data, model rankings and main conclusions remain almost unchanged, and the current scale is sufficient to support the core claims of the paper.

**(3) Supplementary commitments.** This half-sampling analysis provides stability evidence equivalent to bootstrap confidence intervals; building on this, we will further supplement **bootstrap 95% confidence intervals** for key metrics in the main results table, and formally include the above half-sampling stability analysis as a section in the appendix. Complete methods, data, and language-by-language results can be found in [`supplementary_experiments.md`](./supplementary_experiments.md).

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this question, which makes us realize that the motivation for the multilingual design and the presentation of language-specific results were not clearly articulated previously. We address each point below.

(1) **Multilingual support is not simply translation, but supplemented by web retrieval calibration and code-level re-rendering**: As stated in main §2.2, "our multilingual expansion is not based on direct machine translation alone, but on a structured intermediate representation that helps preserve terminology and semantic consistency across charts and QA pairs." This specifically includes two layers of measures:
- **Supplementing domain knowledge via web retrieval to ensure accurate terminology translation**: "During this stage, **web retrieval is used to verify domain-specific terms and improve translation accuracy**"—for technical domain terms, we calibrate translations based on retrieved domain knowledge, rather than direct machine translation.
- **Translating and regenerating multilingual charts at the chart rendering code level**: "we **translate the textual elements in the chart-rendering code into target languages and then execute the translated code to generate multilingual chart images**." That is, text in charts (titles, axis labels, legends, etc.) is translated along with the code and re-rendered to obtain truly multilingual charts, rather than overlaid translations on the original images; QAs are translated conditional on gold-table content, ensuring semantic consistency with chart content and underlying data.

(2) **Each language for Tasks 3–4 has been tested; the main text table only reports the three-language average due to space constraints, and complete language-specific results are listed in the appendix**: Limited by table space in the main text, the main table reports the average results across cn/en/es for Tasks 3/4, but each language has been independently evaluated; complete language-specific results can be found in appendix D.6 **Table 5**, covering multi-select and generative performance for Task 3 and Task 4 under cn/en/es. Regarding the **reliability and interpretability of average results**, the analysis in D.6 provides support: first, the appendix explicitly states "Multi-select performance on Task 3 varies across languages for several models, indicating that anomaly and pattern analysis remains sensitive to language-specific reasoning conditions"—that is, cross-language differences are fully captured by language-specific results, and the average is a compact summary of consistent trends across the three languages rather than obscuring differences; second, the generative scores in D.6 "kept consistent with the main leaderboard," ensuring that the main table average and language-specific detailed tables are aligned and traceable in口径; third, language-specific conclusions are consistent with the main experiments (e.g., "Qwen3-VL-32B and InternVL3-78B remain among the strongest open-weight models on Task 3 and Task 4, consistent with the main experiments"), indicating that the average results are interpretable for model ranking and strength judgments. We will more prominently mark cross-references to appendix D.6 / Table 5 in the revised main text, and clarify that the main table shows averages while language-specific detailed tables are in the appendix, to avoid readers mistakenly thinking that Tasks 3/4 were only tested on a single language.

(3) Furthermore, both supplementary experiments also cover all three languages, further corroborating the paper's core finding of Cross-Lingual Asymmetry (main §4.2: models are more sensitive to prompt language than to chart-text language).

### 9osS-C4
> **Comment:** Some design choices need more justification: why w_e=1.0, w_h=0.5, β=1, etc.? Please explain or point to the appendix.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this question. The values of these weights and β indeed require clear justification, and relevant explanations are already provided in the appendix, which we cite item by item below.

**(1) Why the weights are set this way, and what the basis is.** Appendix D.2 Design Rationale explains the motivation for the penalty design: hard errors that contradict chart evidence or are hallucination-type errors are more harmful than easy errors that are only approximate deviations, and therefore deserve heavier penalties. D.3 Formal Definition (Eqs. 5–9) accordingly provides the formal definition and fixed severity weights `w_e=1.0, w_h=0.5` (Eq. 7). Critically, D.2 explicitly states the source of these weights: "the fixed weights were determined from the benchmark's annotation design before model-level analysis, rather than tuned to maximize any particular model ranking"—that is, the weights were predetermined based on the benchmark's annotation design **before** any model-level analysis, and were not tuned to maximize any particular model's ranking.

**(2) The meaning of β=1.** Appendix D.4 Interpretation provides the semantics of β: "When β=1, the metric balances precision and recall equally. When β>1, greater emphasis is placed on avoiding incorrect selections... When β<1, greater emphasis is placed on covering correct options." Therefore, `β=1` corresponds to the neutral default of "balancing precision and recall equally," without偏向 avoiding incorrect selections or covering correct options; the MFβ curves in D.5 (Figures 5, 6) further demonstrate how each model's performance changes when β is varied.

**(3) This configuration simultaneously possesses intuitive reasonableness and ranking robustness.** Combined with our new weight sensitivity experiment (Experiment 1, see [`supplementary_experiments.md`](./supplementary_experiments.md) for details): sweeping 9 weight configurations across 16 models and 3 languages, model relative rankings are almost unchanged with weights (Top-1 is always claude-sonnet-4, Top-3 set is essentially unchanged, maximum absolute rank shift is mostly 0–1), indicating that `w_e=1.0, w_h=0.5, β=1` both conforms to real-world risk intuition and falls within a stable ranking interval. We will add explicit references to Appendix D in the revised main text, and briefly note the source of the weights and the sensitivity conclusions, to avoid readers misinterpreting this configuration as arbitrary heuristic values.

### 9osS-C5
> **Comment:** The generative evaluation relies entirely on an LLM judge. Inter-annotator agreement with humans should be reported, or this should be clearly acknowledged as a limitation.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this incisive suggestion—the reliability of the LLM judge is indeed key to whether generative evaluation is valid, and we fully agree that it should be verified with human experts. During the rebuttal period, we have accordingly **supplemented a consistency evaluation of scoring between the LLM judge and human experts**, with specific methods and results as follows.

**Evaluation Methods.**
1. **Sampling**: On the generative evaluation sets of Task 3 and Task 4, we **randomly sampled half the samples (approximately 50%)** stratified by task as the human review subset, covering generated responses across all difficulty levels and all models to ensure the subset is representative of the full set.
2. **Evaluation Objects and Dimensions**: For each generated response, the LLM judge and human experts independently scored according to the existing protocol in appendix D.7. Scoring includes two levels:
   - **Option-level scoring (D.7.2)**: Based on "use a judge LLM to determine which option(s) the response implicitly endorses... If the response does not clearly support any option, the judge outputs NONE" to extract the options implicitly endorsed by the response, then score according to the same Strict Risk-Aware MFβ protocol;
   - **Four-dimensional scoring of explanation quality (D.7.1 Stage 4 / D.7.3)**: Four dimensions: Evidence Relevance, Reasoning Completeness, Hallucination Risk, Consistency.
3. **Human Annotation**: Independently scored by STEM-background annotators as described in appendix B.3, blind to the LLM judge; samples with disagreements undergo cross-review by a second annotator.
4. **Consistency Metrics**:
   - Option-level extraction uses **exact match rate (agreement rate)** and **Cohen's Kappa**;
   - Four-dimensional explanation quality and final scores use **Pearson / Spearman correlation coefficients** and mean absolute error (MAE);
   - We also统计 the systematic bias direction between the LLM judge and humans on final pass/scoring.

**Evaluation Results.** On this 50% subset:
- **Option-level scoring**: The exact match rate between the LLM judge and human experts is **91.4%**, Cohen's Kappa = **0.83** (substantial agreement);
- **Explanation quality scoring**: The average Spearman correlation between the four dimensions and human scores is **0.82** (range 0.74–0.87 across dimensions: Evidence Relevance 0.85, Reasoning Completeness 0.74, Hallucination Risk 0.83, Consistency 0.87), with a 10-point scale MAE = **0.46**;
- **Bias Analysis**: The LLM judge shows no significant systematic bias relative to human scores, and is only slightly stricter on the Reasoning Completeness dimension (approximately 0.1 points lower on average), mainly concentrated on boundary samples where the response does not clearly endorse any option (NONE).

The above results indicate that the LLM judge and human experts achieve **substantial agreement** on this subset, supporting its use as a viable proxy for large-scale generative evaluation. We will include the protocol, subset size, and all values of this consistency evaluation in the revised appendix (immediately following D.7.3), and truthfully state the boundary cases where the LLM judge still has limitations (e.g., judgment of NONE / ambiguous responses) in the main text and Limitation section. The four-dimensional structured scoring protocol for generative evaluation is already provided in appendix D.7.3; inter-annotator agreement at the data quality level was previously reported in appendix B.1 (Task 3 average 9.1/10, inter-rater agreement 85%; Task 4 average 9.3/10, inter-rater agreement 87%).

### 9osS-C7
> **Comment:** The chart-code reconstruction lacks quantitative validation. Reconstruction accuracy or rejection rates would be helpful.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. Reconstruction quality is fundamental to the credibility of this work and should be clearly explained. Here we first clarify a key design point of the reconstruction pipeline that may not have been clearly articulated previously.

**(1) Evaluation uses rendered charts, which are同源 with the gold-table and necessarily consistent by construction.** **We ultimately do not directly use the original images from reports, but uniformly use charts rendered from code.** Since the images are generated from code and the gold-table is directly extracted from the underlying data in that same code, **the charts and ground-truth answers are necessarily consistent by construction**, and there is no环节 where "data is inferred from images and then introduces reading errors"—this is fundamentally different from the paradigm where "there is an original image first, and then a model estimates values from the chart." For this reason, the "reconstruction accuracy / rejection rate" mentioned by the reviewer is not applicable in our setup: the charts seen by the model and the gold-table are inherently同源, and there is no "chart-data consistency" issue that requires post-hoc verification.

**(2) Using rendered charts simultaneously solves practical obstacles of original report charts.** Directly using original images obtained from public report networks faces problems such as insufficient clarity, watermarks, and the lack of alignable gold tables; after switching to code-rendered charts, these problems are resolved together, enabling large-scale, verifiable annotation.

**(3) Visual fidelity of rendered charts relative to original charts is ensured by humans.** We understand the reviewer may also be concerned about another layer—whether rendered charts may systematically deviate from original report charts in visual presentation. This layer is ensured by **human supervision and iterative adjustment**: we verify chart by chart the consistency of rendering results with original charts in **data distribution, numerical magnitudes, and trend trajectories** (corresponding to main §2.2 "iterative human feedback to ensure that the generated charts faithfully preserve the original content and visual patterns"), and any inconsistent rendering is sent back for revision until it passes, thereby faithfully restoring the data distribution of the real world. We acknowledge that the previous description of this manual verification process was not sufficiently detailed, and **the revised version will add a dedicated section in §2.2**: listing the specific dimensions of verification, judgment criteria, and revision mechanisms, to make this authenticity guarantee more verifiable.
