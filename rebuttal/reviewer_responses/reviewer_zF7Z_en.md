## Reviewer zF7Z（Rating 5, Confidence 3）

### zF7Z-C1
> **Comment:** The paper emphasizes realism, yet ... charts are reconstructed from public reports, and questions are synthesized mostly via LLMs with human correction. The authors should discuss whether reconstructed charts might systematically differ from original reports in ways that affect task difficulty.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for their careful scrutiny of realism, which is the core selling point we most wish to clarify. Here we first clarify a key design point that may not have been clearly articulated previously: **the charts ultimately used for evaluation are not the original images from reports, but charts rendered from rendering code**, and the gold-table is directly extracted from the underlying data in that same code. Therefore, the charts and ground-truth answers originate from the same code, and the two are necessarily consistent by construction, eliminating the fidelity risk of "chart-data mismatch." Using rendered charts rather than original images also resolves practical obstacles such as insufficient clarity, watermarks, and the lack of alignable gold tables in original images obtained from public report networks, enabling large-scale, verifiable annotation.

We understand the reviewer is actually concerned about another layer—**whether rendered charts may systematically deviate from original report charts in visual presentation, thereby affecting task difficulty**. This layer is ensured by human supervision and iterative adjustment: we verify chart by chart the consistency of rendering results with original charts in data distribution, numerical magnitudes, and trend trajectories, corresponding to main §2.2 "iterative human feedback to ensure that the generated charts faithfully preserve the original content and visual patterns," and any inconsistent rendering is sent back for revision until it passes, striving to faithfully restore the data distribution and chart morphology of the real world. We acknowledge that the previous description of this manual verification process was not sufficiently detailed, and **the revised version will add a dedicated section in §2.2**: listing the specific dimensions of verification (data point values, trend directions, chart types and coordinate ranges, etc.), judgment criteria, and revision mechanisms, and including a qualitative discussion of "whether rendering-original chart differences affect task difficulty," to make this authenticity guarantee more verifiable.

### zF7Z-C2
> **Comment:** Scale is small for a benchmark: With 2,160 QA pairs per language, the per-task sample size is approximately 540.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for their attention to data scale. We understand that 2,160 QAs per language, approximately 540 per task, may not appear large in absolute terms; this scale is primarily determined by the higher annotation and quality control costs of multi-chart reasoning instances—unlike single-chart QA, each instance requires establishing verifiable reasoning chains across multiple charts and undergoing complete manual revision and quality inspection. It should be noted that, in terms of QA count for horizontal comparison, our scale is comparable to or even larger than contemporary benchmarks (exceeding ChartQAPro's 1,948 and MultiChartQA's 2,000).

However, we also agree that the key to scale is not absolute quantity, but **whether it is sufficient to support stable and reliable conclusions**. To this end, we have specifically supplemented a **half-sampling stability experiment**: stratified random sampling of 50% of samples by task (i.e., approximately 270 per task), repeated 100 times, yields Spearman correlation ≥ 0.986, Top-3 overlap rate 0.98–1.00, and mean rank shift < 0.4 positions for model rankings across all three languages. This indicates that even with half the data, model rankings and main conclusions remain highly stable, and the current scale is sufficient to support the core claims of the paper. Building on this, **the revised version will supplement bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for key metrics in the main results table**, and formally include the above half-sampling stability analysis as a section in the appendix, so that the sufficiency of scale has quantifiable evidential support.

### zF7Z-C3
> **Comment:** Human performance baselines lack detail, such as the annotation protocol, number of annotators, inter-annotator agreement, and annotator qualifications.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for pointing this out. The details the reviewer is concerned about—annotation protocol, number of annotators, inter-annotator agreement, and annotator qualifications—are actually already relatively comprehensively covered in Appendix B of the paper, but may not have been sufficiently signposted in the main text. We cite them item by item below and explain how we will strengthen them:

- **Number and qualifications of annotators (appendix B.3)**: "We recruited four annotators with undergraduate degrees or above in STEM-related fields to ensure sufficient expertise in chart interpretation and logical reasoning." That is, 4 annotators with STEM bachelor's degrees or above to ensure professionalism in chart understanding and logical reasoning; the appendix also specifies the $15/hour compensation and informed consent arrangements.
- **Annotation protocol (appendix B.1)**: Tasks 1–2 adopt full cross-review of the complete dataset—"each item was independently checked by another annotator in a cyclic peer-review process," with review focusing on three aspects: Question validity, Reasoning correctness, Answer accuracy; Tasks 3–4 undergo four aspects of human revision for model-assisted synthesized samples (Question-type alignment, Validity of correct options, Effectiveness of distractors, Explanation consistency), ensuring the final result is "human-refined benchmark instances rather than raw model outputs."
- **Inter-annotator agreement (appendix B.1, Post Hoc Quality Audit)**: A 30% random subset of Tasks 3/4 is subjected to expert quality inspection, showing "Task 3 achieved an average score of 9.1/10 with an inter-rater agreement of 85%, while Task 4 achieved an average score of 9.3/10 with an inter-rater agreement of 87%."
- **Human performance baselines (appendix B.2)**: The expert group answered without access to ground truth, achieving scores of 97.83 / 94.83 for Tasks 1/2, 90.60 / 91.60 for Tasks 3/4 in the multi-select setting, and 85.80 / 87.50 in the generative setting; the original paper also notes that "the more complex tasks still led to a non-negligible error rate, highlighting the intrinsic difficulty... of MultiChartQA-R," corroborating task difficulty from the human side.

We acknowledge that this information is scattered in the appendix with insufficient signposting in the main text. **The revised version will add explicit cross-references to appendix B in the experimental section of the main text (at the human baseline), and supplement the human baseline evaluation details specifically mentioned by the reviewer**—including whether responses were independent, whether there were time limits, evaluation order, and the domain distribution of annotators, to make the reproducibility of human baselines clearer.

### zF7Z-C4
> **Comment:** Qualitative analysis of substantive reasoning failure is absent ... what kinds of cross-chart integration or causal reasoning models consistently get wrong. The error analysis just focuses on prompt-format sensitivity and label bias.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this constructive suggestion. We agree that the existing error analysis (appendix E.5, Tables 14–15) mainly stays at the **statistical level**—reporting option omission rates and multi-selection rates of four commercial models on Tasks 3/4, and据此 observing that "models generally perform better at eliminating incorrect options than at recalling all correct options" (appendix D.7.2 / E.5), i.e., models are better at eliminating incorrect options than at fully recalling correct options; this is indeed insufficient to reveal specifically where models go wrong in **substantive reasoning**.

However, several analyses already in the paper provide preliminary clues for "what types of reasoning models consistently get wrong," which can serve as a basis for qualitative analysis: (1) **Failure of discriminative perception / relevance filtering**—the irrelevant chart experiment in appendix E.1 (Table 6) shows that after adding thematically related but logically useless distractor charts, strong models such as Claude are almost unaffected (70.00→69.11), while InternVL3-78B drops sharply from 73.21 to 60.93 on Trend Inference, indicating that some models struggle to filter out "seemingly related but actually useless" information across multiple charts; (2) **Failure of cross-lingual reasoning rather than perception**—the cross-lingual asymmetry in appendix E.3 / §4.2 indicates that the bottleneck lies in instruction following in the reasoning module, not visual OCR. These all point to the "cross-chart integration and interference rejection"环节, rather than mere format sensitivity or label bias.

Building on this, we fully accept the reviewer's suggestion, and **the revised version will add a new section "Qualitative Analysis of Substantive Reasoning Failures" after appendix E.5**: from the error cases of Tasks 3/4, we will categorize and organize typical samples by failure type—such as cross-chart evidence integration errors (omitting or mismatching information from a particular chart), causal/trend misjudgments, contradictions between evidence and conclusions, and being misled by the "seemingly rigorous reasoning" of hard distractors, etc.—and provide the proportion and representative cases of each type. We would also like to add a favorable condition: we **fully retained the reasoning outputs of each model during evaluation, including intermediate Chain-of-Thought reasoning chains** (the evaluation itself uses CoT prompting, see main §3.1), so we can directly localize step by step based on these reasoning processes which环节 the error occurred in—"chart reading → cross-chart integration → inference → conclusion"—rather than inferring backwards from the final option alone. This enables us to conduct much deeper error analysis than option statistics. We believe this can advance error analysis from "option statistics" to "reasoning process diagnosis," more directly addressing the reviewer's concern.
